**What is showing** · SOCAN's suit, the Isbell class action and Round Hill's $1bn claim landed in the same week, and the battleground is shifting from training inputs to output similarity. Output similarity can only be litigated with measurement tooling, which Korean rightsholders do not yet have.

SOCAN, the Canadian music rights society, filed suit against the AI music generation platform Suno in federal court on September 2. According to [Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/now-canada-socan-sues-suno-claiming-it-illegally-copied-hits-like-both-sides-now-and-sk8er-boi/), the claim covers 150 songs allegedly copied without authorization, including Joni Mitchell's "Both Sides Now" and Avril Lavigne's "Sk8er Boi." Whether the complaint identifies specific generated outputs as resembling specific originals is not clear from the reporting I checked, so I will not call this a pure output-infringement case. I will read it as a copying claim with an output stage possibly folded in. A Jason Isbell class action and a Round Hill suit seeking one billion dollars were reportedly filed around the same time, though I could not confirm the plaintiff structure or the relief sought in either.

Once the output stage becomes the contested ground, someone has to decide how closely a generated track resembles the source. Both the "Blurred Lines" verdict and the Ed Sheeran "Thinking Out Loud" case measured similarity through an ordinary listener standard, and whether the output of an attribution algorithm would be admitted as evidence remains untested. The usefulness of a measurement tool shows up at the negotiating table before it shows up in a ruling, because licensing terms need a number that can be split song by song.

Warner Music has reportedly agreed to acquire the AI attribution startup Sureel. In my draft I wrote a completion date of June 10, 2026, which was an error, a future date written in the past tense with no source behind it. I am leaving the timing and terms unverified. If the acquisition is real, it looks less like buying a vendor and more like owning the formula that sets the terms. The opposite path is also open. If deals settle the way Universal and Udio reportedly did, catalog-wide, on a flat fee or an equity stake, per-track similarity drops out of the center of the calculation.

IFPI's Asia regional director Mira Chatt has named attribution, compensation, consent and transparency as the conditions for voluntary licensing. Attribution without a tool stays a declaration. Splitting a pool eventually requires counting whose material went in and how much.

Korea runs on a three-rights structure covering authors, performers and producers, with play counts serving as the shared ruler. KOMCA (Korea Music Copyright Association (한국음악저작권협회)) and KOSCAP (함께하는음악저작인협회) outsource broadcast monitoring and fingerprinting, but fingerprinting identifies identical recordings and cannot measure how much a new song resembles an old one. My bet is that within 180 days at least one of those two societies, or one of HYBE, SM, YG or Kakao Entertainment, publicly adopts a similarity or attribution tool, or announces a partnership or investment in a vendor building one. Society press releases and label disclosures will settle it. A tool alone changes nothing if the distribution rules have no line item for AI outputs, and rule amendments move on general assembly schedules. The task for next week is not tool evaluation but finding the clause number where an AI usage fee would sit.

Any industry facing generative substitutes should ask who is building the similarity scale before arguing about the rate.
