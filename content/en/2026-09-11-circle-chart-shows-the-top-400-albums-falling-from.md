**What is showing** · A survey where 71% of Gen Z rated print magazines as more authentic than digital, plus film cameras, vinyl and flip phones resurging simultaneously across multiple categories

On September 9, [Kyunghyang Shinmun](https://www.khan.co.kr/article/202609091104001/) (Kyunghyang Shinmun) ran a piece on teenagers and early-twenties buyers moving back toward physical things. The article cited a survey finding that 71 percent of Gen Z consider print magazines more authentic than digital. That number is real but differently sourced: it comes from the 2025 "Return of Touch" study by Quad and the Harris Poll, and the respondents were consumers overall, not Gen Z specifically. What they said was that printed catalogs and magazines felt more authentic than digital campaigns. Film cameras, vinyl and flip phones showed up in the same cluster of returning objects.

Calling this nostalgia breaks down immediately. Someone born around 2010 has no memory of owning a flip phone. The Kyunghyang piece borrows the word anemoia, longing for a time you never lived through, to name that gap. If there is no memory to miss, the pull is not toward an era but toward a property these objects share. A roll of film ends at 24 or 36 exposures, sometimes 12 by Lomography's count, and you cannot go back and delete a frame you already shot. A record makes you stand up for the B side. A magazine fixes the order of its pages. Time goes in from the user's side, and the maker's time stays visible in the object.

Walter Benjamin argued that mechanical reproduction strips the aura from the original. Once reproduction is the default, the aura does not vanish so much as the baseline slides back a notch. Computer graphics were rejected on arrival for having no human hand in them, and after generative AI arrived, the same CG gets cited as evidence of labor. The 71 percent reads less like a verdict on paper and more like a signal that things on screens have become too cheap to carry weight.

Korean entertainment's physical album business is already past its peak. Circle Chart year-end data reportedly shows combined domestic sales of the top 400 albums falling from around 119 million copies in 2023 to around 98 million in 2024. Almost all of that volume points at fandom, and photocards arrive attached to albums. What the object proves in that case is a purchase record. The hours spent making it rarely appear on the product page. The money a teenager spends on one roll of film sits on the opposite side of that.

So the bet: within 180 days, at least three Korean entertainment companies or magazines will release a physical format aimed at teenagers as a standalone product rather than bundled merch. Zines, photobook-format magazines, limited cassette or vinyl runs. Verification is new release announcements plus standalone product pages in their own stores, not album bundles. One caveat: if distribution stays inside fandom platforms, standalone still collapses into fan sales, so watch for general bookstores and select shops too.

A practical test for tomorrow. Delete the member names and the free-gift notice from your product copy, and see whether the remaining sentences explain how the thing was made. If nothing remains, it is merch, and fandom sets the price.

Any manufacturer competing against infinitely copyable alternatives should start writing the production process into the product page, because that is the part a copy cannot carry.

**Bet** · At least three Korean entertainment or media companies will launch physical formats for teens - zines, magazine-style photobooks, limited cassette/vinyl - as standalone products rather than bundled merch. · within 180 days · check: New release announcements from major agencies and magazines, and whether standalone product pages appear in their online stores

**Principle** · When frictionless infinite copies become the default, consumers start paying for the time and handprints behind the object rather than the object itself - for the first time, there's a reason to make products less smooth.
