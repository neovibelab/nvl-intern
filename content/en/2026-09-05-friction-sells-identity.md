Cai Xukun (蔡徐坤)'s physical album "KUN" sold 120,000 copies shortly after release, according to Chinese outlet 36Kr. The same report translated the revenue math: one vinyl copy earns roughly what 20,000 to 30,000 streams would. The timing of that story matters. It landed right after the RIAA's 2024 U.S. music revenue report showed vinyl outselling CDs for a third straight year, and after IMARC's research counted roughly half of new 2024 vinyl buyers as aged 18 to 34. Streaming made listening as easy as pressing one button, and the group buying back the least convenient format is the youngest one in the market.

Carlotta Lodven, writing in Jing Daily, laid out how luxury brands manage desire without discounting it. Hermès lets staff judgment decide who gets to buy. Chanel uses waitlists and price increases. Neither lets a customer complete a purchase instantly. Streaming had erased that kind of delay entirely; vinyl brings back the order, the wait, the act of placing a record on a turntable. But it's too early to say that sequence alone creates desire. What matters is whether a buyer chose that friction themselves or whether a seller built it in advance.

Korea's album market draws that line clearly. Labels insert random photocards into every album and place fan-meeting raffle tickets inside only certain versions, a structure designed specifically to make fans buy the same album many times. Circle Chart's yearly sales rankings show why: per-buyer purchase counts routinely run into the dozens or hundreds for the same title. Here, the friction isn't something a fan absorbs to prove their own commitment. It's something the industry installed to raise revenue. A Cai Xukun vinyl buyer and a K-pop fan buying ten copies of one album both count as physical-format consumption, but one is clearing scarcity on their own, and the other is receiving scarcity that was pre-portioned for them.

IFPI's 2024 global report named Korea as the market with the highest share of physical revenue worldwide, and noted that a large part of that revenue comes from repeat purchases aimed at photocards and raffle entries. Korean labels have already converted friction into an engineered probability reward rather than leaving it to organic desire. What travels from the Cai Xukun case isn't a lesson to add more friction everywhere. It's a filter for telling which friction a customer proves themselves through and which friction was simply planted by the seller. Within the next six months, Korean retailers such as Kyobo Hottracks or Synnara Records, or press coverage citing their data, should start naming 20-to-30-year-olds as the core group actually driving vinyl purchases, not just album purchases.

Any industry selling both a convenient and an inconvenient version of the same product should check which of its own friction points customers are choosing and which ones were quietly built in to inflate the count.
