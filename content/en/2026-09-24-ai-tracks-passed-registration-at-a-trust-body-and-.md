# AI Tracks Passed Registration at a Trust Body, and Collected Royalties

**What happened** · On September 22, a policy discussion was held at the National Assembly on AI music copyright management standards. The meeting discussed including AI-generated music with substantial human contribution in authoring, composition, and arrangement in copyright trust registration, and disclosed cases of illegally collected royalties totaling 16.3 billion won between January 2024 and July 2025.

---

On September 22, six Korean music rights organizations held a joint forum at the National Assembly Members' Office Building in Seoul on how to set registration standards for music made with AI. Four discussants spoke, drawn from music publishing, a distribution platform, an AI detection company and academia, according to News1. At the forum it was reported that AI-generated tracks had already passed registration at a trust body and collected royalties ([Asiae](https://view.asiae.co.kr/article/2026092210450758579)). A trust body is the organization a songwriter hands rights to so it can collect fees from broadcasters, shops and streaming services and pay them back out. The registration certificate is effectively a deposit slip, so once screening is beaten, money simply leaves.

That those tracks were not caught means the finished audio file tells you nothing about how much of a person went into it. What the screening desk receives is a sound file and a declaration form, and neither carries any trace of the making.

So the object of review slides down from the song to the work record: the project file from the recording software, the log of prompts typed into the AI tool, the edit history. At the forum, the proposal discussed was to exclude what Koreans have started calling "one-click music", where someone types a command and takes the output, while imposing a penalty of three times the amount wrongly received on anyone who files a false declaration. Verifying every log is impossible, so the design instead lowers the expected payoff of lying until keeping records becomes the cheaper option.

My bet: within 180 days, either the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism's follow-up forum materials or the revised registration rules of KOMCA (Korea Music Copyright Association (한국음악저작권협회)), the main Korean collecting society, will contain a clause requiring creators to submit or retain this kind of process evidence. The place to check is the published text of the amendment.

The obstacles are plain. Logs can be faked, and for a solo producer, keeping a paper trail is pure cost with no upside. KOMCA's own standard, discussed for three years in its AI committee, took effect on August 3 and was voted down 22 days later ([Nate](https://m.news.nate.com/view/20260825n32698)). That was less about the content than about who had the authority to decide. The ministry recommended withdrawal on August 18, already-registered AI works were asked to cancel, and pending applications were put on hold ([Asiae](https://view.asiae.co.kr/article/2026082608485585092)). Until someone is trusted to make the call, more paperwork will only mean more paperwork.

Korean production companies and labels have two things to settle now. Whether freelance songwriting and arrangement contracts will carry a duty to disclose AI use and a retention period for work files, and from what date in-house sessions and prompt histories start being kept. Records created after the standard lands cannot be backdated.

Any field where the finished output can now be synthesized, from lab results to insurance claim photos, will find that the notebook, not the product, becomes the thing being examined.

**Prediction** · The forthcoming AI music trusteeship standard from MCST and KOMCA will require submission or retention of creation-process evidence such as session files, prompt logs, or edit histories. · within 180 days · check: Check the published draft of KOMCA's revised registration rules and MCST follow-up forum materials for a process-evidence clause

**Takeaway** · When an output can no longer be authenticated on its own, verification moves upstream to the production record-and whoever kept the log keeps the rights.
