# UMG Confirmed the New Platform Will Be 'Distinct and Separate' From ElevenLabs' Own

**What happened** · ElevenLabs and Universal Music Group signed a licensing agreement to jointly develop an AI music production platform. The platform enables fans to participate in remixing and mashups while returning revenue to artists. Medellín Music Lab is recruiting for a music industry talent development program.

---

Universal Music Group signed a multi-year licensing deal and strategic partnership with the AI audio company ElevenLabs on September 10, 2026, and the first product from it will be a co-developed platform where fans remix licensed songs and create personalized vocal versions, [Variety](https://variety.com/2026/music/news/umg-elevenlabs-ai-powered-music-platform-licensing-1236857240/) reported. The timing was pointed. Suno had unveiled a model backed by Warner Music, Believe and BMG the day before. ElevenLabs closed a $500 million Series D at an $11 billion valuation this year, up from $6.6 billion at an internal tender offer five months earlier.

What UMG actually negotiated is less the right to use the catalog than the line where that use stops. UMG confirmed that the new platform will be "distinct and separate" from ElevenLabs' existing music product, according to Music Ally, and Music Business Worldwide reported that ElevenLabs released its own model, ElevenMusic v2.5, on September 11, two days after Suno's v6. So the licensed playground and the general-purpose engine ship as two different things, on purpose. That separation is the deal, not a footnote to it. The same shape appeared in UMG's October 2025 agreement with Udio, which described a subscription platform built inside a licensed and protected environment. Every time a rights holder has moved from litigation to licensing, part of the price has been product separation.

Music Ally has described the industry's contradiction plainly enough: labels want licensed AI, and they do not want an unlimited generation engine flooding streaming with low-value tracks. The stitching that holds both positions together is feature scope plus disclosure. My bet is that when the UMG and ElevenLabs platform launches, within 180 days, it will not offer open text-to-song generation. It will be limited to remixing and vocal transformation inside a designated catalog, and outputs will carry AI labeling. The check is simple and public: the feature list on the product page and the disclosure clause in the terms of service at launch. Where I could be wrong is if Udio, backed by the independent-label agency Merlin, opens broader capability outside the fence first, and UMG loosens its own limits to keep pace.

For a label, distributor or any rights holder weighing a similar agreement, two lines on the contract matter more than the headline rate. First, whether permission can be split by use, so that lyrics and instrumental material are open for training while a named artist's vocal timbre stays closed. Second, whether the separation between the model you licensed and that company's other models is written down, covering both training data and shipped products, rather than left to a press statement.

Any industry licensing its archive to an AI vendor, from stock photography to broadcast news to medical imaging, should read this as the template: price the boundary, then price the data.

**Prediction** · The UMG-ElevenLabs platform will launch limited to remixing and vocal transformation within a designated catalog rather than open text-to-song generation, with AI labeling on outputs · within 180 days · check: Check product page and ToS at launch for feature scope and AI-disclosure policy

**Takeaway** · When rights holders partner with tech, the real negotiation is over boundaries, not price: defining where usage stops protects asset value more than defining what may be used.
