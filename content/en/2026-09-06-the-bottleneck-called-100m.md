BTS wrapped its Arirang world tour with 38 shows, and according to [Asiae (Asia Economic Daily)](https://view.asiae.co.kr/article/2026083017333834136) the run grossed roughly 599.8 billion won on 2.3 million tickets, with one eight-show stretch alone bringing in 140 billion won. NH Investment & Securities reportedly put the total economic effect of what it calls BTS-nomics at around 8 trillion won. The number that will outlast the headline sits lower in the same report: in Billboard's first-half touring tallies, BTS was the only K-pop act averaging more than $10 million per show.

Divide the tour figures and the shape gets clearer. About 15.8 billion won per night, roughly 60,000 people in the building, about 260,000 won of gross per attendee. At 1,450 won to the dollar that is a shade over $10.9 million, and the eight-show stretch runs hotter at 17.5 billion won per night. What I could not verify is the denominator. I do not know how many K-pop acts appear in that ranking at all, or whether the ones below the line sit at $8 million or $2 million. This argument stands or falls on that missing column.

Alan Krueger's Rockonomics described how the recorded-music collapse pushed the industry to patch the hole with ticket prices, and how those higher prices concentrated at the very top. K-pop ran the same sequence late, because member-version albums and photocards kept lifting spend per fan long after streaming had flattened it elsewhere. Albums scale with how many copies one fan buys. Touring stops at what a city will bear, and premium packages are simply price discrimination aimed at the fans willing to pay most. An act without recognition has nothing to attach them to.

Run the arena math. Ten thousand seats, an average ticket of 120,000 won and 800 million won of fixed cost per night puts break-even near 6,700 seats, about 67 percent. Add 30,000 won of merchandise per head and it falls to the mid-5,000s. For an act whose add-on revenue cannot cover a quarter of per-show fixed cost, adding dates multiplies the loss by the date count.

So here is my forecast, not an observation. When HYBE, SM, JYP and YG report third-quarter results in November, I expect top-tier concert revenue and per-seat pricing to rise while tour announcements issued between August and November show mid-tier groups doing at least two of three things: cutting dates, moving to smaller venues, or swapping primary markets for secondary Asian cities. If none of the three appears, I was wrong and will log it that way.

The counterargument I cannot yet defeat is that venue and logistics costs rose from post-pandemic demand generally, not from stadium K-pop tours. Separating the two requires comparing rental inflation in cities that hosted these tours against cities that did not.

Any industry with a record year should check how many firms hold the top unit price, because a boom carried by a few reaches everyone else as cost.
