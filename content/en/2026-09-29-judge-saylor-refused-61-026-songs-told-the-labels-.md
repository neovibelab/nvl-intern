# Judge Saylor Refused 61,026 Songs, Told the Labels to Bring a Separate Case

**What happened** · Warner Music Group announced leadership changes including Val Blavatnik's appointment and Simon Robson's departure. Suno admitted its v6 model was trained on user-generated content while countering UMG and Sony's copyright lawsuits. Sony Music joined ARIAM as the first music company in an AI policy alliance.

- **Suno** · An AI music generation platform that creates complete songs with vocals, lyrics, and instrumentation from text prompts. Developed by Suno, Inc. of Cambridge, Massachusetts and launched publicly in December 2023. The AI music generator that Universal Music and Sony Music sued.
- **Jack Brody** · Chief Product Officer at Suno, overseeing product and design for the AI music generation platform. Joined from Snap in November 2024. Suno's chief product officer who stated that v6 was trained from scratch.
- **DistroKid** · A U.S. digital music distribution company that helps independent artists deliver music to 150+ streaming platforms including Spotify and Apple Music. Founded in 2013 by Philip Kaplan. A distribution service that Universal Music sued separately.
- **BMG** · A German-American music company combining music publishing and record label operations that manages music rights. A major label that struck a deal with Suno.
- **Believe** · Paris-based music distribution and artist development company founded in 2005, owner of TuneCore platform. A distribution company that struck a deal with Suno.

**Why this one** · Court filings revealed that a single distributor supplies more than half the tracks on a major streaming platform. Anyone planning a distribution or catalog business now has to recalculate where low entry barriers actually concentrated power-and who bears liability for it.

---

Universal Music and Sony Music filed a 45-page second complaint against the AI music generator Suno in a Massachusetts federal court on 18 September, [Variety](https://variety.com/2026/music/news/sony-music-universal-music-sue-suno-label-backed-model-1236866921/) reported. The filing exists because Judge F. Dennis Saylor IV had, on 18 August, refused to let the labels bolt 61,026 additional songs onto their first lawsuit and told them to bring a separate case instead, as [Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/from-warner-musics-leadership-shake-up-to-sunos-courtroom-defiance-its-mbws-weekly-round-up/) summarised.

The dullest explanation for a song list growing by tens of thousands is discovery, the pre-trial stage where each side must hand over internal documents to the other. Once Suno's files opened, the labels could count more. Counting capacity, not indignation, is what expanded.

What the new complaint actually stakes out is not the size of the list but the generation of the software. The labels describe Suno's models up to version 6 as fruit of the same poisonous tree, meaning products derived from earlier copying they say was unauthorised. Suno's chief product officer Jack Brody told MBW that v6 was trained entirely from scratch and contains no data from the two companies. Both sides are now using the same measuring stick: which model version absorbed which material. Damages fall out of that reading. If the copying is found to be wilful, US law allows up to $150,000 per song, which MBW calculated as roughly $9 billion in theory.

Warner Music is not on this axis at all. It settled with Suno in November 2025 and signed a partnership, and BMG and Believe struck their own deals, Bedroom Producers Blog reported. Two of the three major labels are filing their claim lists with a court. The third handed a similar list to a commercial counterparty instead.

For rights holders outside the United States, the more consequential document is a distribution contract. In Universal's separate suit against DistroKid, a service that uploads independent artists' songs to streaming platforms for a fee, MBW reported that more than half the tracks on one major streaming platform came through DistroKid. Universal did not sue the individual musicians who uploaded. It sued the company that runs the pipe. Responsibility and, more importantly, records accumulate first at the intermediary. An artist who cannot independently verify which model generation ingested their work has exactly one instrument available, which is a contract clause obliging the distributor to produce that record.

Korean collecting societies, the bodies that gather royalties on behalf of songwriters and pay them out, calculate on plays and uses. That logic has nothing to say about training contribution, because a song used once to teach a model is not a play.

Any industry whose suppliers feed data into someone else's system should read this as a contest over bookkeeping rights rather than over money.

**Takeaway** · When a dispute looks like a fight over damages, what is actually being settled is who owns the ruler that measures contribution - read a rising claim count as growing measurement capability, not growing anger.
