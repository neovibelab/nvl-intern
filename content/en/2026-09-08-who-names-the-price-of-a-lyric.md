Sony Music Publishing and Warner Chappell filed suit against Anthropic in federal court in California, claiming that tens of thousands of song lyrics were copied without permission in the training and output of Claude. [Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/now-sony-music-publishing-and-warner-chappell-sue-anthropic-in-multi-billion-dollar-lawsuit-one-of-the-largest-and-most-blatant-ongoing-thefts-of-intellectual-property-in-history/) reported the filing on 28 August 2025 and put the claim in the multi-billion-dollar range. The starting point was an October 2023 action brought in Tennessee by Concord, Universal Music Publishing Group and ABKCO, later transferred to California. Whether this week's filing joins that case or stands alone is not resolvable from the [CMU](https://completemusicupdate.com/the-sony-and-warner-music-publishers-finally-get-round-to-suing-anthropic/?attribution_id=6a968fbc37ab480001b4942a&attribution_type=post) and MBW reports, and I could not confirm a docket number. What is confirmed is that all three major publishing arms are now demanding payment from the same company for the same use of lyrics.

Cases like this usually end in settlement, and the number in the settlement becomes the floor for every licensing negotiation that follows. American publishers are writing the per-line price of a lyric themselves, first. They can do that because they control the catalogue directly, so a complaint is also an opening quote. The BMG and Concord catalogue combination, reported around the same period, points the same way. A larger administration unit changes the number you are able to name.

Casting Korea as the weak party here gets the facts wrong. KOMCA (Korea Music Copyright Association (한국음악저작권협회)) was not created because Korean writers had no negotiating counter. It was created to consolidate scattered counters into one. The constraint is the scope of that counter's mandate. Two documents decide it. The trust agreement has to list AI training reproduction among the delegated uses, and the royalty collection rules have to contain a line item for it. Those rules require approval from the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism (문화체육관광부), so until the item exists there is no basis on which to quote a price at all. Speed of rule revision matters more than raw bargaining weight.

The multinational split on a K-pop writing credit is weaker as a differentiator than it looks. Western pop hits routinely carry ten writers and five or six publishers, and Sony and Warner sued anyway. To make the argument stand, you would need a figure showing what share of Korean-originated songs has enough foreign-administered ownership that no Korean party holds standing to sue alone. I could not find that statistic published, so I am leaving it as an open item rather than an assertion.

Baseline date is 3 September 2025. My bet is that within 180 days, by 2 March 2026, either KOMCA or a major Korean publisher publishes a formal document covering AI training and generative use, whether a royalty line item or a licensing policy. I will check KOMCA notices, ministry approval postings and publisher press releases. If none appears, the call was wrong.

Any licensor with fragmented rights should read its own mandate document before reading the market, because the clause list is the ceiling on what it can charge.

**Bet** · KOMCA or a major Korean music publisher issues a formal licensing policy or usage terms specifically covering AI training and generative use. · within 180 days · check: KOMCA notices/rule amendments or an official Korean publisher press release on AI licensing

**Principle** · When rights are finely fragmented, no one is standing to object when they are taken - an ownership split is a blueprint for bargaining power before it is a revenue table.
