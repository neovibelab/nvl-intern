# Warner and Believe Feed One Model, Two Definitions of Consent

**What happened** · BMG and Concord completed their merger on September 1, creating the fourth-largest major in music. Sony Music Publishing and Warner Chappell sued Anthropic over copyright infringement, and SOCAN sued Suno. Suno launched its v6 model on September 9 in partnership with WMG, BMG, and Believe under licensing agreements.

---

Suno released three models on September 9, v6, v6-wild and v6-mini, and retired everything that came before them. According to [Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/suno-v6-ai-music-models-launch-in-partnership-with-wmg-bmg-and-believe/), the new models were trained on licensed data supplied by Warner Music Group, BMG and Believe. The launch landed inside a two week window of litigation. Sony Music Publishing and Warner Chappell sued Anthropic in the Northern District of California on August 28, and Canada's SOCAN filed against Suno in Federal Court on September 2, per MBW and BNN Bloomberg citing the Canadian Press. The same week, BMG closed its merger with Concord on September 1 to form the industry's fourth largest major, which MBW rounded up [alongside the lawsuits](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/from-bmg-and-concords-completed-merger-to-anthropics-multi-billion-dollar-lawsuit-its-mbws-weekly-round-up/).

Read in sequence, the suits did not block the licenses. They set the price. [Rolling Stone](https://www.rollingstone.com/music/music-features/ai-war-suno-music-industry-partner-bmg-1235607385/) reported that BMG's August deal with Suno licensed its full catalog and simultaneously resolved claims over past unauthorized use. That is the Udio route, where a settlement with UMG and WMG converted into a licensing arrangement, now repeated. The ceiling keeps rising as it repeats. The Anthropic music case started at roughly 500 songs in October 2023, grew to 20,000 works and a $3bn demand in January, and now covers tens of thousands of songs with statutory damages of up to $150,000 per work plus $25,000 for each removal of copyright management information, according to Engadget and MBW. Each settlement becomes the floor for the next advance.

What stays unresolved is the counting unit. Warner Music has not disclosed how much catalog went into v6, or whether its artists can opt out, per Rolling Stone. Believe and TuneCore, in their September 8 agreement, specified artist opt-in and compensation. Two suppliers feeding one model, two different definitions of consent. That gap is why Jason Isbell and three other artists filed a right of publicity class action in Massachusetts federal court on August 31, as Billboard reported. A corporate license does not do individual math.

So the bet: within 180 days, none of WMG, BMG or Believe will itemize training attribution in quarterly filings or artist statements, and payments will show up only as catalog level advances and revenue shares. The clearest way for me to be wrong is Believe and TuneCore, which already hold an opt-in roster and could open track level accounting first.

For anyone in Korea negotiating with an AI music company, the number to check is not the advance. It is two lines. At what unit training usage is reported, catalog, track or artist. And whether the rightsholder has a contractual right to verify that figure. Germany's GEMA published concrete infringement examples and SOCAN adopted the same method, Music Ally reported, which shows the measuring tool can be built from the rightsholder side too.

Any industry licensing its archive into a model, from stock photo libraries to news publishers, should negotiate the audit clause before the rate.

**Prediction** · Under Suno's v6 licensing regime, per-artist training attribution and payout methodology stays undisclosed, handled only as catalog-level advances and revenue shares · within 180 days · check: Check whether WMG/BMG/Believe quarterly filings or artist statements itemize a 'training attribution' line, and MBW/Billboard reporting on opt-in artist payouts

**Takeaway** · In a market without rules, litigation is price discovery, not punishment - whoever gets sued and settles first ends up writing the industry's standard rate.
