# Sanrio Is Putting 10 Billion Yen Into Development

**What happened** · At TGS2026, non-gaming companies including Sanrio Games, Toei (東映)ゲームズ, and PARCO entered game publishing using their own IPs. Sanrio targets 10 game releases and 10 billion yen in revenue by 2029. Japan's home game market exceeded 700 billion yen in 2025, with online sales accounting for approximately 70%.

**What is showing** · TGS2026 set up a forum session solely on non-game companies entering game publishing, with Sanrio, Toei, PARCO and Shueisha Games each exhibiting under their own publishing labels.

---

On September 17, 2026, at the Tokyo Game Show business day forum, three companies that do not make games shared a stage under the title "A Challenge from Other Industries": Sanrio, the retail developer PARCO, and the film studio Toei, as reported by [Nikkei Cross Trend](https://xtrend.nikkei.com/atcl/contents/18/00607/00100/). Sanrio had already launched its own label, Sanrio Games, on April 21, with a plan to ship ten titles by March 2029 according to Nihon Keizai Shimbun. Toei Games exhibited at TGS for the first time with three titles including *HINO*, per Gameview, and is reportedly distributing a Steam demo from September 17.

Follow the money and this does not look like a revenue play. ZDNet Korea and The Japan Times report Sanrio is putting 10 billion yen into development and marketing. Until now the company lent its characters to outside studios and collected royalties, which is income with no production risk attached. Giving that up to carry a 10 billion yen development exposure needs an explanation, and executive director Hamasaki Kosuke (皓介) (濵﨑Kosuke) gave one at the briefing. Games are an "entrance to Sanrio," and the point is to extend the total hours fans spend in contact with the IP through content people stay inside for a long time, as covered by Game Watch. The stated target is time, not sales.

A royalty returns revenue but leaves the hours with the licensee. So do the play logs, the accounts, and the data that tells you what to sell next. The first title, *Sanrio Party Land*, packs more than 145 of the company's 450-plus characters and over 45 minigames, according to Denfaminicogamer, which reads less like game design than like spreading the whole catalogue across one screen. Merchandise and theme park tie-ins were announced alongside it.

Buying contact time means buying failure cost too. Under licensing, a weak game damages the brand only partly, because the licensee's name absorbs some of it. In-house publishing removes that buffer, and the Japanese console market is not a forgiving place to learn: it passed 700 billion yen in 2025 with roughly 70 percent of sales already digital, which means these publishers are competing on storefronts where discovery is brutal.

I still think the pattern continues. Shueisha Games is already on the TGS2026 exhibitor list. My bet is that by March 2027, at least one more Japanese non-game IP holder from character goods, publishing, film, or retail will formally announce either a new in-house game publishing label or its first self-published title. I will check company press releases and the TGS2027 exhibitor list.

For any company that licenses out characters, webtoons, or music rights, the next contract deserves one more line beside the royalty rate: who keeps the user's play time and account data after the deal ends.

**Prediction** · By March 2027, at least one more Japanese non-game IP holder (character, publishing, film or retail) will officially announce a new in-house game publishing label or its first self-published title. · within 180 days · check: Company press releases and the TGS2027 exhibitor list

**Takeaway** · Licensing returns revenue but never returns the hours your customer spends with you. In businesses where contact time is the asset, decide who owns that time before you negotiate the royalty rate.
